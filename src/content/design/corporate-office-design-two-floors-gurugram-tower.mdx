## The brief

One company taking two floors of an office tower in Gurugram: directors, finance, HR and
sales, with the meeting load and the amenities that go with them.

Two floors in the same building is a different problem from two floors in two buildings.
The lifts, the core and the structure are fixed and identical, so the useful question is
not how to make each floor distinctive — it is **how much of one floor to repeat on the
other**.

## Two floors, one logic

Both plans do the same three things:

- **Cabins and director rooms along the glazed edge**, where the daylight is.
- **Departments in the middle**, in low-partitioned blocks: finance and HR on one floor,
  sales and accounts on the other.
- **Services at the core** — lifts, staircase, shaft, toilets — with reception facing them.

Repeating that logic is worth more than variety. Anyone who learns one floor already knows
the other: the conference room is off reception on both, the pantry is by the core on both,
and the toilets are in the same place. What changes is only which departments sit where,
and which floor carries the main conference room.

Dimensions are drawn in feet and inches, as most fit-out drawings in this market still are:
the conference room at roughly **21 ft 11 in by 14 ft**, the meeting room at **11 ft 2 in by
10 ft 2 in**, an HR department block at **11 ft 3 in by 8 ft 10 in**, reception at about
**13 ft by 17 ft** with a built-in seat rather than a separate waiting room.

## Drawn to elevation level

This is the part worth borrowing from, whoever designs your office.

Each principal room here is drawn twice: a **furniture plan**, and then **every wall it has**
as elevations A, B, C and D, with the finishes marked on each one — fluted panel, dark
veneer, light panel, metal beading, laminate, veneer with a mural pattern. The conference
room and the MD cabin both get a full sheet.

The difference is not presentational. A rendered view tells you the intent; an elevation
tells a carpenter where the joint falls, how tall the panelling runs and which side the
beading sits on. **Where only views exist, those decisions get made on site by whoever is
standing there** — and they are then paid for twice, once in material and once in
rework.

It is also how you find problems early. Drawing the MD cabin's four walls is what forces
the questions of where the safe goes, how the planted corner is served, and whether the
door swings into the visitor chair.

## Partitions: three types, on purpose

The plan marks three different partition systems, and the mix is the design:

| Where | What | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cabins and conference room | Full-height partition, veneer finish | Acoustic separation for rooms where confidentiality matters |
| Department blocks | Low-height partition, mica finish | Defines a team without sealing it, and keeps sightlines across the floor |
| Reception, meeting room edges | Glass partition, slim aluminium profile in a rose-gold PVD finish | Borrows daylight from the glazed edge into the middle of the plate |

Using one type everywhere is what makes an office feel either sealed or exposed. The
drawings also mark an **access-controlled door** into one department, and **borrowed
ventilation** for the internal toilets — both decisions that have to be made with the
services layout rather than after it.

## The rooms most plans leave out

**A pooja room with its own door.** About 14 feet across, off the reception end, with a
backlit niche and a stone platform. It is on the plan from the start, which is the only way
it ends up as a room rather than a shelf in a store.

**An executive lounge.** Panelled, with armchairs and a low table, for the conversation
that happens before a meeting starts or after it ends. In its absence that conversation
occupies the conference room for another half hour.

**A sculpture niche at the lift lobby**, marked on the plan. A small thing, but it means
the first thing a visitor sees leaving the lift was designed rather than left over.

## Cafeteria, pantry and the open floor

The **cafeteria takes one long table** instead of scattered fours — it seats more people in
the same area and reads as one room. The **pantry is separate**, so tea service through the
day does not cross lunch.

On the open floor, **storage units topped with planting** do the screening instead of desk
partitions: privacy at seated height, greenery at standing height, and one piece of
furniture doing two jobs. Where the plate runs deep and daylight thins out, **wall graphics
and a textured plaster finish** give that side of the floor something to look at.

## What to settle before construction drawings

- **Which floor gets the main conference room**, and therefore which floor visitors reach.
- **Department adjacencies** — finance next to HR is a different floor from finance next to
  sales, and moving a department later moves partitions and power.
- **How much is drawn to elevation level.** Every room is ideal; the principal rooms are
  the minimum.
- **Access control**: which doors, and who administers them.
- **Ventilation routes for internal rooms**, agreed with the services drawings.
- **Whether both floors are built together.** Phasing changes procurement and the site
  sequence more than it changes the design.
