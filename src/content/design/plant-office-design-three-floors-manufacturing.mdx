## The brief

The office block inside a working manufacturing plant, over three floors. It had to hold
the management team, the dispatch function, the meeting load a plant actually runs, and
the amenities a shop floor needs — without any of those four crossing each other.

That last point is the whole design. In a city head office you plan around the lift lobby
and the window line. In a plant office you plan around **who has to reach whom, and who
must not walk through what**.

## What a plant office needs that a corporate office does not

**Dispatch needs the ground floor and a window.** It runs on interruption — drivers, gate
passes, loading queries — and if it sits upstairs, every one of those interruptions walks
through the office. Here it stays at ground level, facing the yard it coordinates.

**The plant head needs to be able to leave.** A cabin at the far end of a floor looks
senior and works badly: when a line stops, the walk is the cost. The cabin sits on the
management floor with a direct route down, and it has its own meeting table, so a review
happens in the room instead of migrating to the conference room.

**Visitors and auditors need a route that stops short of production.** Waiting, a meeting
room and the toilets are all reachable at ground level. An auditor can be received,
seated and briefed without a floor pass — which matters on the days that decide whether a
customer audit goes smoothly.

**The canteen serves two populations on different clocks.** Office staff eat over an hour;
a shift eats in twenty minutes. Putting the canteen on the top floor with the training
room and breakout keeps that surge above the management floor rather than through it.

**A war room, not another meeting room.** Production review is daily, short and
repetitive. Given a small room of its own, it stops consuming the conference room every
morning.

## How the three floors stack

| Floor | Holds | Why here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ground | Reception and waiting, visitor meeting room, dispatch office | Everything an outsider touches, plus the one function that must see the yard |
| First | Open-plan workstations, director and plant head cabins, conference room, discussion room, war room | The working floor, above the visitor route and below the amenity traffic |
| Second | Canteen, training room, breakout | Used in bursts, noisy, shared with the shop floor — so kept clear of the floor below |

Read the stack as a traffic diagram rather than a hierarchy. Outsiders stop at ground
level. Shop-floor staff pass the first floor to reach the second, on stairs placed for
that. The management floor is the only one with no through traffic, which is the point.

## The four meeting spaces, and why each exists

A plant does not need six identical rooms; it needs rooms of clearly different sizes, or
every one of them ends up booked for a two-person call.

- **Conference room, sixteen seats** — full management reviews and customer meetings that
  come inside.
- **Discussion room, eight seats** — the default working meeting.
- **War room, six seats** — the daily production review. Small on purpose.
- **Ground-floor meeting room** — visitors, vendors and auditors, with no floor entry.

The cabins carry the overflow, which is why the plant head's and director's cabins both
have tables rather than only a desk.

## Finishes

The palette is deliberately plain and hard-wearing: large-format floor tiles that survive
trolleys and boots, gypsum ceilings with recessed lighting, blinds on every window wall
because a plant office usually faces an unshaded yard, and glazed cabin fronts so the
open plan keeps its daylight.

The only decorative move is on the walls — graphic panels in the meeting rooms, dispatch
and breakout. In an industrial building that is the cheapest way to make a floor feel
finished, and it is also the easiest thing to change later, which suits a room whose use
may shift.

## What to settle before construction drawings

- **The visitor and audit route**, marked on the plan. If it is not drawn, it will be
  improvised, and it will cross the floor.
- **Shift timings and headcount per shift**, which size the canteen. A canteen sized on
  total headcount is either half empty or a queue.
- **Where dispatch sits relative to the gate and the weighbridge.** Moving it later moves
  partitions and data cabling.
- **Server and electrical room locations**, and who needs access to them out of hours.
- **Glare control on the yard-facing side.** West light off a concrete yard is harsher
  than the same elevation in a city.
- **Whether the training room doubles as a canteen overflow.** If it does, its furniture
  and power need to allow for it from the start.
