## The brief

A small single-floor office for an investment business: one run of workstations, a
supervisor, a managing director's cabin and a board room. That is the whole brief — and it
is a harder design problem than a large floor, not an easier one.

On a large plate a mistake can be absorbed somewhere else. Here there is no somewhere
else. **Every room is on the only route through the office**, and nothing can be moved
later without moving a partition.

## The plan, dimensioned

| Room | Size on plan |
| --- | --- |
| Workstation run | 28 ft × 10 ft |
| Supervisor's desk | 8 ft × 6 ft |
| MD cabin | 10 ft × 8 ft 6 in |
| Board room | 14 ft 3 in × 10 ft |
| Main passage | 6 ft 5 in wide |
| Entry passage | 5 ft wide |
| Secondary passage | 4 ft wide |

The passages are the part worth copying. **A passage has to let someone walk past a seated
chair without asking the person in it to move** — below about 3 ft 6 in it stops doing
that, and in a small office there is no second route around. Giving the main passage 6 ft
5 in here costs desks, and is still the right trade.

The plan also marks low-height cabinets along the length of the bay and a TV niche in the
board room wall, which is how storage and equipment stay out of the furniture layout.

## Spend where the impression is made

In a small office a visitor sees three things: the entrance, one passage, and the room they
are taken into. So **the board room carries the money** and everything else is kept simple
and well made.

That shows up as three decisions in this study:

- **Ring pendants over the table.** A horizontal line of light at eye level does more for a
  room this size than any number of downlights.
- **Full-height panelling on three sides**, so the room reads as finished from every seat —
  not just from the head of the table.
- **An upholstered panel wall opposite the timber.** This is the acoustic move, and it is
  the one most often dropped: a board room with hard surfaces on every side is unusable on
  a video call, which is now most of what a board room does.

## The supervisor sits on the floor

There is one cabin in this office, and it is the MD's. The supervisor sits at the head of
the workstation run, facing it.

A cabin for one supervisor costs a partition, a door, a light fitting and about 48 sq ft —
space a small office cannot spare. It also removes the one person whose job is to see what
is happening on the floor. An 8 ft by 6 ft desk placed at the head of the bay does the
same job and gives the room an obvious front.

## Storage as wall finish

In a room of ten by eight and a half, furniture standing away from the walls is the enemy.
So the MD cabin takes **full-height storage** on one side and a **low credenza** along
another; the workstation bay takes **low-height cabinets** for its whole length.

Built in, storage becomes the wall finish. Bought later, it becomes a steel cupboard in the
corner of the room a visitor sees first.

## Daylight down the bay

The desks run **perpendicular to the glazing** rather than facing it. Facing a window puts
the brightest thing in the room directly behind a screen, and the usual result is a blind
that stays permanently closed — the office then loses the daylight it paid rent for.

Turned side-on, the same glazing lights the full length of the bay, and the small meeting
table at the window end gets the best seat in the office.

## What to settle before construction drawings

- **Headcount at capacity, not today.** Adding two desks to a 28 ft run is easy; adding a
  fifth room is not.
- **What the board room is really for.** If it is mostly video calls, the acoustic and
  camera-facing wall matter more than the table.
- **Where the server and the printer live.** In a small office both end up in the passage
  unless they are drawn.
- **Storage volume in running feet**, agreed before joinery is priced.
- **Whether the office will be handed back.** A short lease favours demountable partitions
  over drywall.
