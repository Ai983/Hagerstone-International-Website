## The brief

A standalone canteen building on a manufacturing site, feeding three groups from one
kitchen: the shop floor, the staff, and the management table. The plan had to work at the
moment a shift breaks — which is the only moment a canteen is really tested.

A canteen is not a room with tables in it. It is a **flow**, and the plan either lets 200
people through it in twenty minutes or it does not.

## Sizing: seats are set by the sitting, not the headcount

The building here carries **200 seats in the workers' hall, 120 in the staff hall and 16
at the directors' table**. Those numbers come from the biggest group eating at once, not
from total employment.

This is the decision that most often gets made backwards. A site with 600 people across
three shifts does not need 600 seats; it needs enough for the largest shift, *if* that
shift eats in one sitting. Stagger the break into two sittings and the hall halves — but
the servery then has to move twice the trays in the same total time, which means a second
serving line, more bain-marie length and more hand-wash points. **The break pattern
decides the building.** Settle it before the plan, not after.

## The flow, entry to plate return

One direction, never crossing itself:

1. **Enter and wash.** Hand-wash areas sit at every entry — 3340 × 3040 mm at the staff
   entrance, 2090 × 2600 mm near the directors' end, and another at the workers' entrance.
   Put the basins inside the hall instead and the queue forms across the dining floor.
2. **Collect.** Hot and cold bain-marie counters with a service counter between them.
3. **Sit.** The halls sit directly off the servery, so nobody carries a tray past a queue.
4. **Return the plate.** Plate collection, dish racks and pot wash are grouped together
   **behind** the servery, with a bin-dry zone and the garbage route leaving from the same
   back-of-house side.

The last point is the one that gets designed wrong most often. If the plate return is next
to the serving queue, every person finishing a meal walks against every person starting
one, twice a day, for the life of the building.

**Circulation is drawn at 1.2 m** for the internal passages, and a common pathway runs
along the service side so kitchen traffic never enters a hall. **Air curtains** at the
entries and **flap curtains** at the kitchen openings keep flies and kitchen heat out of
the dining halls — cheap at construction stage, impossible to add gracefully later.

## Three halls, three furniture strategies

| Hall | Size on plan | Seats | Furniture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Workers' dining | 24150 × 9255 mm | 200 | Fixed bench tables with stools on a frame |
| Staff dining | 11961 × 12015 mm | 120 | Loose tables and chairs, plus high tables at the edge |
| Directors' dining | 11900 × 5000 mm | 16 | One long table, plus a lounge seating corner |

The furniture follows how the room is used, not the status of the people in it. **Fixed
benches in the workers' hall** mean nothing to move, nothing to go missing, and a floor
that can be washed down in one pass. **Loose furniture in the staff hall** because that
room is also used for briefings between meals. The **directors' room is screened with
slatted timber rather than walled off**, so it reads as part of the same building.

A **tuck shop of 2440 × 2000 mm** sits between the halls, a **cafe counter with high
stools** serves the break that does not need a tray, and **drinking water coolers** are
placed at each hall rather than centrally.

## Behind the servery

An Indian canteen kitchen needs more than a Western layout assumes, and this plan is
specific about it: a **chapati-making area** with hot plates and a puffer, a **burning
area** with burners and a rice steamer, a **preparation area**, a **dry store** with
racks, a cutlery and crockery cabinet, and a separate **pot wash with pot storage**.

The **gas bank sits outside the building**, reached from the service side and agreed with
the site's safety team — never a decision to leave to the kitchen contractor. A
**janitor's closet**, a **dorm room of 2785 × 2945 mm** for the resident kitchen staff, and
separate toilets for men (4700 × 3000 mm), women (3690 × 1680 mm) and the directors' end
complete the back of house.

## Finishes, and why they are dull on purpose

Every surface here is chosen to be washed: large-format floor tiles laid to fall, a brick
dado up to sill level so trolleys and stools do not mark plaster, and concrete with
grooves at the entrance so wet footwear has somewhere to lose its water.

The building is a **sandwich-panel roof at lintel level** on a simple frame, with
**clerestory glazing above the brick dado**. That band of high glass is the quiet move in
the whole scheme: it lights a deep hall all day without putting sun on the tables, so the
lights stay off through the shift.

## What to settle before construction drawings

- **The break pattern and the biggest sitting.** Everything else is downstream of it.
- **The menu, honestly.** Chapati and rice in quantity need different equipment, different
  extract and different serving lengths.
- **Serving lines: one or two.** Two sittings almost always mean two.
- **The waste and delivery route**, and where bins stand between collections.
- **Water supply and drainage capacity** for the wash lines, which are the heaviest
  services load in the building.
- **Whether the dorm room is really needed**, and if so, its access out of hours.
- **Gas bank position**, agreed with the site safety team before the layout is frozen.
