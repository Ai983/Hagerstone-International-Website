## The brief

An indoor children's play zone with a family cafe, over a base level and a mezzanine.

Every other study in this section is planned around people at work. This one is planned
around **two people at once**: the child, who decides whether the place is any good, and
the adult, who pays, waits, and decides whether to come back.

Design for only the first and you get a venue parents endure. Design for only the second
and you get a cafe with a slide in it.

## The floor, zoned by noise and by age

The base level takes everything loud and fast: the pretend-play street, a construction
zone, RC car and cycle arenas, a super climber, a trampoline room and the aeroplane
experience. The mezzanine takes everything quiet: a library, a lego and wooden-block area,
sand and art tables, and a play farm.

That split does three jobs at once. It keeps a four-year-old on a ride-on vehicle away
from a toddler on a mat. It gives the venue somewhere to put a birthday party without
stopping general admission. And it means the noisiest hour of a Saturday is contained on
one level rather than filling the whole volume.

Service rooms — kitchen, kitchen wash, store, toilets, changing and the lift — sit along
one edge on both levels, so staff never cross a play zone to reach them.

## The street is the wayfinding

The centrepiece is a pretend-play street: shopfronts for a garage, a fuel station, a
grocery, a clinic, with kerbs, zebra crossings, traffic lights, street lamps and a clock
tower. A train runs on a track above it, and a cloud ceiling hangs over the whole thing.

**A street does what signage cannot.** A child reads it instantly and knows where
everything is; each shopfront becomes its own activity without needing four separate
rooms; and it gives parents a single sightline down one axis instead of a maze to search.

It also ages well commercially: a shopfront can be re-themed in a weekend, while a fixed
play structure is what you are stuck with for a decade.

## Toddlers need a threshold, not a sign

Soft play and the toddler area sit off the main circulation, with a raised mat edge and a
low gate. The point is physical: a crawling child cannot drift into a ride-on route, and
nobody is relying on a parent reading a rule.

Inside the zone, **bench seating for adults** — because the parent of a two-year-old is
within arm's reach the whole time, not sitting in the cafe. Padded discs go at toddler
height, everything has a radius rather than an edge, and the floor is a cushioned mat.

## The cafe is the business

Admission has a ceiling. Food and drink do not.

So the cafe takes the **arched window line** rather than a back corner: daylight,
terrazzo, timber tables, pendant lighting and a spiral stair up to the play level. A
parent who can sit somewhere pleasant with a view out stays longer and orders twice, and a
ninety-minute session becomes an afternoon.

The **cloud forms from the play floor carry into the cafe ceiling**, which is what stops
the venue reading as two unrelated businesses sharing a door.

## Finishes, judged by the cleaning trolley

Everything below about 1.5 m gets touched, kicked and wiped every day, so nothing there
should be textured, porous or delicate.

| Where | Specified as |
| --- | --- |
| Cafe and circulation | Terrazzo, for wear and for washing |
| Play zones | Resilient sheet flooring with padded mats where children fall |
| Walls to 1.2 m | Washable paint or laminate, nothing fabric |
| Themed joinery | Laminated ply with radiused edges and no exposed fixings |
| Ceiling features | Lightweight moulded forms, hung clear of services access |

The themed joinery is where these projects are won or lost. Bought as furniture, a play
venue looks like a catalogue; **built as joinery, it looks made** — which is the difference
a parent notices without being able to name it.

## What to settle before construction drawings

- **The age range you are actually selling to.** A venue for 2–6 and a venue for 4–12 are
  different buildings, and trying to be both halves each.
- **Ceiling height under the mezzanine**, which decides what can hang and what cannot.
- **Party rooms: how many, and can they be closed off** without shutting the floor.
- **Peak occupancy**, which sets toilets, the cafe and the staff count more than the play
  equipment does.
- **Who maintains the themed elements**, and whether spares are held for the pieces that
  will break first.
