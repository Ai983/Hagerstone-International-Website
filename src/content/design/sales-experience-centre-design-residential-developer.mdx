## The brief

A sales experience centre for a residential developer — the building where buyers are
received, shown the project, taken through a floor plan and, if it goes well, closed.

That makes it an unusual brief. An office is designed for the people who work in it every
day; an experience centre is designed for a stranger's first twenty minutes. The staff
accommodation is real and has to work, but it is fitted around the visitor's route rather
than the other way round.

## Every room sized on plan

The study fixes room sizes and seat counts before any finish is chosen, which is the right
order: a 1000 × 2100 mm table needs a room of a certain size, and finding that out later
is how meeting rooms end up with chairs that cannot be pulled out.

| Room | Size on plan | Seats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cafe and lounge | 9200 × 6800 mm | 14 chairs, 5 tables, 2 sofas |
| Reception | 6200 × 4800 mm | 2 |
| Conference room | 4800 × 6800 mm | 11–12 around a 1200 × 3750 mm table |
| Meeting room one | 3300 × 5400 mm | 6 around a 1000 × 2100 mm table |
| Meeting nook | 2100 × 2700 mm | 3 |
| Sales head's cabin | 2500 × 3400 mm | Desk plus 2 visitors |
| Sales workstations | 5200 × 5800 mm | 9 desks, 6 pedestals |
| Private dining | — | 16 seats across 4 tables |
| Security cabin | — | 1 |

Read the meeting mix from the bottom up. **The three-seat nook is the room that will work
hardest**, because most property conversations are one family and one salesperson. Without
it, that couple ends up in the twelve-seat conference room, which changes the tone of a
negotiation more than any finish does.

## The arrival sequence

Three things have to happen in the first minute: the visitor sees the project, someone
greets them, and they are given somewhere to sit that does not feel like a queue.

So the **scale model sits in the arrival sightline but out of the walking route** — visible
on entry, walkable all the way round, without crossing the reception desk. The **cafe does
the waiting**, with proper seating and a coffee counter rather than a row of chairs; a
family with children will wait there without minding, and the salesperson gets a second,
less formal place to talk. Both the model and the cafe get dedicated lighting, for the
reason below.

## A lighting plan per room

This is the part most fit-outs skip, and in a sales building it is the part that decides
whether the finishes read as premium. Each room in the study carries its own plan rather
than a general ceiling grid:

- **A pendant over every table.** Focused light on the surface where the conversation and
  the paperwork happen.
- **Recessed downlights for the ambient level**, so the room is evenly lit when the
  pendants are the only feature.
- **Wall washers on the feature walls.** Textured wallpaper, fluted panelling and stone
  only look like anything when light rakes across them. Lit flatly, an expensive wall
  reads as beige.
- **Warm white at 3000K throughout**, which keeps timber and stone warm. Cooler light
  makes the same materials look grey and the room look like a clinic.
- **Anti-glare fittings at the sales desks**, plus linear pendants over the bay — staff sit
  under these all day, which is a different requirement from a room a visitor uses once.
- **A statement chandelier at reception and in the cafe.** One per space, as the focal
  point; the rest of the lighting in those rooms is deliberately quiet.

## Finishes

A sales centre is a showroom for the developer's own standard of finish, so the palette
leans warm and tactile rather than corporate:

| Element | Specified as |
| --- | --- |
| Floor, work areas | Carpet tile in a textured loop pile |
| Floor, front of house | Italian marble, with engineered wood in the cafe |
| Joinery and tables | Wood laminate in a walnut tone, matte black metal bases |
| Feature walls | Textured plain wallpaper, fluted oak panelling, marble-pattern panels |
| Ceilings | Gypsum with paint, dropped and blacked out where the lighting needs to disappear |
| Window treatment | Fabric blinds on every glazed elevation |
| Hardware | Satin stainless door furniture, heavy-duty floor springs on the glass doors |

Two choices are worth calling out. **The walnut laminate is used on every table, desk and
counter** — one tone across the building, so the joinery reads as a set rather than a
collection. And the **ceilings are painted out in black where the track and pendant
lighting sits**, which makes the fittings read as intentional instead of as clutter.

## Outside the door

The study carries the arrival sequence out to the site, because a visitor forms an opinion
before they reach reception. The facade uses **ACP in a cocoa teak wood finish** with
travertine and textured paint, mosaic tile at the entrance, and paved driveway and parking
blocks. The planting is chosen for structure and low maintenance in full sun —
bougainvillea for colour, foxtail and travellers palms for height, cycas and bismarck palm
for form.

## What to settle before construction drawings

- **Peak visitor load on a launch weekend**, not an average day. It sizes the cafe, the
  parking and the number of small rooms.
- **Whether the model is final.** Its footprint sets the arrival layout, and models are
  often built after the interior is drawn.
- **How long the centre will stand.** A sales centre with a three-year life takes different
  partitioning and services decisions from a permanent building.
- **Where staff park and enter.** If sales staff arrive through the visitor entrance, the
  arrival sequence is compromised every morning.
- **Signage and branding zones**, so wall finishes and lighting are coordinated with them
  rather than cut into later.
