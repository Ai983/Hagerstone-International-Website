## The comparison as it is usually framed — and why that is wrong

Most discussions treat this as glass being the expensive premium option and
drywall the sensible economical one.

That framing misses the two things that actually decide it: **where the services
go**, and **how many times this floor will be rearranged.** Get those right and
the answer is usually obvious.

## Side by side

| Factor | Glazed partition | Drywall partition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daylight through the floor plate | Passes through | Blocked |
| Sound insulation potential | Good with double glazing and seals | Higher, easier to build up |
| Services in the wall | Not possible | Power, data, switches concealed |
| Cost per square metre | Higher | Lower |
| Reconfiguration | Demountable, reusable | Demolished and rebuilt |
| Waste at churn | Low | High |
| Programme | Fast — dry, pre-made components | Board, joint, sand, paint, dry |
| Wet trades | None | Jointing and painting |
| Appearance change | Fixed system aesthetic | Any paint or finish |
| Privacy | Needs manifestation or film | Inherent |

## Acoustics: the detail that decides satisfaction

Every acoustic complaint about a meeting room traces back to one of three
things, and the partition material is rarely the main one.

1. **The partition stops at the ceiling.** Sound travels over the top through
   the ceiling void. Either take the partition to the slab, or build an acoustic
   barrier above it.
2. **Junctions are not sealed.** A continuous gap at floor, head or abutment
   defeats a high-performance panel entirely.
3. **Services pass straight through.** A ducted or ceiling-grid path through the
   wall line is an open acoustic route regardless of what the wall is made of.

Sound insulation is rated under **ISO 717-1**, and a laboratory rating describes
a construction installed correctly. What arrives on site is only as good as the
junctions. Where confidentiality genuinely matters — an MD's cabin, an HR room,
a board room — the specification should address the whole build-up including
above the ceiling, not just the panel.

## Services routing: plan it before you choose

A drywall partition is a convenient container. Switches, sockets, data outlets
and small pipework live inside it and nobody thinks about it.

Choose glazed partitions and all of that has to go somewhere else — into the
raised floor, the ceiling void, or a designed post or pedestal. That is entirely
workable, but only if it is decided during design. Discovered on site, it
produces surface trunking on a glass wall, which is the visible sign that this
conversation happened too late.

## The churn argument

This is where the cost comparison genuinely shifts.

A drywall layout is effectively permanent: changing it means demolition, waste
removal, rebuilding, jointing, painting and disruption to a live floor. A
demountable glazed system can be taken down and reassembled in a new
configuration, often over a weekend.

On a floor with a stable long-term layout, drywall's lower first cost simply
wins. On a floor expected to be reorganised every two or three years — a growing
company, a shared floor, a business with changing team structures — the
reusable system starts paying back from the first move.

## Mistakes that cost real money

- **Stopping partitions at the ceiling and expecting privacy.** The most common
  and most expensive-to-fix mistake in this whole subject.
- **Choosing glazed partitions without planning services.** Surface trunking on
  a glass wall undoes the reason the glass was chosen.
- **Lining the facade with solid-walled cabins.** It takes daylight away from the
  entire open plan behind them.
- **Assuming a partition provides fire compartmentation.** Only a tested, rated
  construction does, correctly installed with rated penetrations.
- **Forgetting manifestation on glass.** Full-height glazing needs visible
  markings so people do not walk into it, and it should be designed rather than
  added as stickers afterwards.
- **Ignoring the reconfiguration horizon.** The cheapest wall to build is often
  the most expensive wall to move.

## Standards referenced

Sound insulation rating to **ISO 717-1**; fire and life safety, including
compartmentation and escape provisions, in **NBC 2016, Part 4**; detection
system arrangement to **IS 2189**. Whether a partition must be fire-rated, and
what the escape arrangements require, is determined for each project by the fire
consultant and the authority having jurisdiction.
