## Why this choice reaches the board

Most fit-out overruns are not caused by bad pricing. They are caused by a
**contract structure that did not match how settled the design was.**

A finance director who has approved a number wants that number to hold. A
project lead who knows the layout will change until late wants the flexibility
to change it without a negotiation each time. Those are different instruments,
and choosing the wrong one is what produces the final account that nobody
expected.

## The structural difference

| Factor | Turnkey (lump sum) | Item-rate (schedule of rates) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is fixed | The total price for a defined scope | The unit rate for each item |
| Quantity risk | Contractor | Client |
| Design maturity needed | High — scope must be complete | Moderate — rates survive change |
| Variations | Renegotiated, low leverage | Measured at agreed rates |
| Client admin effort | Lower | Higher — measurement must be checked |
| Cost visibility | One number, limited breakdown | Element-by-element |
| Best when | Design is frozen, budget is fixed | Design is evolving, control matters |
| Risk allowance | Priced in, paid regardless | Not priced in |

## The part people miss: leverage moves after signature

Before signature you have competitive tension — several contractors want the
job. After signature you have one.

Under **item-rate**, that shift matters less, because the rates for common items
are already agreed. A change that adds twenty square metres of partition is
measured and paid at a rate you competed on.

Under **turnkey**, the same change is a fresh negotiation with a single party
who knows you cannot easily go elsewhere mid-project. This is why turnkey
rewards a complete scope and punishes a vague one.

## Where each one genuinely fits

**Turnkey fits** a floor that is fully drawn and specified, a board-approved
budget that must not move, a client team without the capacity to measure work,
and a programme where speed matters more than line-item visibility.

**Item-rate fits** a project where the layout will keep developing, work behind
existing finishes where quantities cannot be known until opening up, a client
who wants to see what each element costs, and any job where a professional will
be checking measurement.

## Measurement is the hidden requirement

An item-rate contract is only as good as the measurement behind it. That means
naming a method — **IS 1200** is the standard reference for building works in
India — and agreeing how each item is measured before work starts, not while
invoices are being argued.

The recurring failure is an item-rate contract signed without anyone on the
client side available to verify quantities. At that point you have accepted
quantity risk and given up the control that was supposed to compensate for it.

## Mistakes that cost real money

- **Signing turnkey against an incomplete scope.** Every gap becomes a variation
  priced without competition. This is the most expensive mistake on this page.
- **Assuming the lump sum is the final figure.** It is final only for the scope
  defined. Read what is excluded as carefully as what is included.
- **Choosing item-rate with nobody to measure.** Control you cannot exercise is
  not control.
- **Leaving the rate schedule incomplete.** Any item missing from the schedule
  becomes a negotiation — the exact problem item-rate was chosen to avoid.
- **Comparing bids across different structures.** A turnkey price and a measured
  estimate are not comparable numbers, and putting them side by side in a board
  paper misleads everyone reading it.

## Standards referenced

**IS 1200** sets the method of measurement for building and civil engineering
works in India, and is the usual reference point for defining how item-rate
quantities are measured. The commercial terms themselves are a matter for the
contract and for legal advice.
