  - question: "What is the correct installation sequence?"
    answer: "Generally: structure and any strengthening first, then main ductwork because it is largest and least flexible, then sprinkler pipework which needs falls and fixed head positions, then cable containment which can route around, then lighting, then the ceiling. Services that cannot easily change direction go first. Reversing this means the flexible trades install first and the inflexible ones then have nowhere to go."
  - question: "How much time does poor coordination cost?"
    answer: "More than the coordination itself by a wide margin. On a fit-out the ceiling is usually the middle third of the programme, and it is where an uncoordinated floor loses weeks."
  - question: "Who should own coordination?"

  - question: "Does the layout affect MEP coordination?"
    answer: "Substantially. Every enclosed room needs its own air supply and return path, its own lighting circuit and controls, and changes sprinkler and detector positions. ## Why this is where programmes slip

The ceiling void is the most contested space in a building. Several trades need
the same volume, in a fixed order, and none of them can see what the others will
do until it is drawn.

Get it right and the middle of the programme runs smoothly. Get it wrong and
work is installed, removed and reinstalled while every following trade waits.

## What competes for the void

| Service | Flexibility | Constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural downstands | None | Fixed; often the binding limit |
| Main ductwork | Low | Large, rigid, cannot easily change level |
| Sprinkler pipework | Low | Needs falls; head positions fixed by layout |
| Drainage from pantries | Low | Gravity — needs continuous fall |
| Condensate drains | Low | Gravity, plus trap depth |
| Cable containment | Medium | Can route around, within bend limits |
| Lighting | Medium | Position fixed by the reflected ceiling plan |
| Ceiling suspension | — | Needs clear hanger routes |
| Access allowance | — | Sacrificed first, regretted later |

The ordering principle is simple: **the least flexible service gets the space it
needs first.** Gravity services and large ducts before cable trays.

## Draw the section, not just the plan

Plans hide the problem. Two services can look fine in plan and occupy the same
height.

The reliable method is sections at the **tightest points** — typically where a
main duct crosses a downstand beam near the core. That intersection, not the
typical condition, sets the achievable ceiling height across the floor.

This is also why a ceiling height should never be promised before coordination
is done.

## The layout drives the services

A fit-out layout is not just a furniture arrangement. Every enclosed room:

- Needs supply air and a return path
- Needs its own lighting circuit and control
- Moves sprinkler heads and detectors
- Cuts and adapts the ceiling grid
- Adds partition head details that services must pass or avoid

So a layout with sixteen rooms is a materially bigger MEP job than the same
floor with six, and a late layout change invalidates coordination that was
already complete. Freezing the layout is an MEP milestone as much as a design
one.

Provisions sit across **NBC 2016, Part 8** for services, **Part 9** for
plumbing, **Part 4** for fire and life safety, with electrical installation
practice in **IS 732** — and what applies to a specific project is for the
project's consultants and the authority having jurisdiction.

## Ownership is the failure point

The most common cause of an uncoordinated floor is not incompetence. It is that
**nobody was made responsible**.

Each subcontractor has drawings for their own service. Each has resolved their
own routes. None has resolved the interaction, because that is not any one of
their jobs.

Name the coordinating party, give the deliverable a date before installation
starts, and require sign-off from each trade on the coordinated set.

## Access is part of the design

Anything that needs maintaining needs reaching: filters, valves, dampers, drain
traps, isolators, terminal units.

An access panel "somewhere in that area" is not access. Panels belong directly
under the item, sized for the work, and recorded on the as-built drawings so the
facilities team can find them in three years.

## A practical checklist

- Is the layout frozen? Coordination cannot finish before it is.
- Are sections drawn at the tightest points, not typical ones?
- Is the installation sequence agreed and issued to every trade?
- Has each trade signed off the coordinated arrangement?
- Are access panel positions on the drawings?
- Are gravity services — drainage, condensate — proven to have continuous fall?
- Does the coordinated arrangement still leave the promised ceiling height?
- Will any change to a unit's position trigger a re-check of its drain route?

## Standards referenced

Building services provisions in **NBC 2016, Part 8**; plumbing in
**NBC 2016, Part 9**; fire and life safety in **NBC 2016, Part 4**; electrical
installation practice in **IS 732**. System design, sizing and the requirements
applying to a specific project must be established by the project's MEP and fire
consultants and the authority having jurisdiction.
