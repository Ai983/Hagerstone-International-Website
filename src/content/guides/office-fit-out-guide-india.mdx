## Who this is for

Someone who has taken, or is about to take, a floor, and now has to turn it into
a working office — and who will be held to a date and a budget.

It is written to be useful before you appoint anyone, because the decisions that
determine how a fit-out goes are made before a contractor is on site.

## The eight stages

| Stage | What it decides | Usual failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Brief and headcount | Everything downstream | Growth assumption never stated |
| 2. Building survey | What you are working with | Assumed instead of verified |
| 3. Design and layout freeze | The date the programme starts from | No named decision-maker |
| 4. Approvals | When work may begin | Treated as paperwork, started late |
| 5. Procurement | Whether the programme holds | Long-lead items released late |
| 6. Site works | The build itself | Services sequence not coordinated |
| 7. Commissioning | Whether it performs | Compressed when the programme slips |
| 8. Handover | What you can operate | Documentation missing |

## 1. Brief and headcount

Everything begins with how many people, doing what, and how that changes.

A brief that says "120 people" is incomplete. The useful version states the
growth assumption, the ratio of desks to people if working patterns are hybrid,
how many enclosed rooms are genuinely needed and for what, and which
departments must sit near each other.

The single most consequential line is the **growth assumption**, because a
layout planned for today's headcount forces a reconfiguration inside the same
lease.

## 2. Survey the building rather than assuming it

Before a layout is drawn, establish what the floor actually offers:

- **Slab-to-slab height**, and what the ceiling void must carry — this sets your
  finished ceiling height and whether a raised floor is possible
- **Existing services capacity** — electrical supply, cooling capacity, fresh air
  provision, drainage points for pantries and washrooms
- **What the landlord provides**, scheduled in writing rather than described as
  "warm shell"
- **Structural constraints** — column grid, downstand beams, core position
- **Access** — lift sizes, loading times, what can physically be brought in

A survey is cheap. Discovering the cooling capacity is inadequate after the
layout is fixed is not.

## 3. Design and the layout freeze

Design proceeds from concept to a frozen layout. The freeze is the milestone
everything else hangs off, and it is an organisational event more than a
technical one.

Two principles worth holding:

**Do not line the facade with enclosed rooms.** It takes daylight from the whole
floor behind them. Place enclosed rooms toward the core, or glaze them.

**Test the services before fixing the plan.** Subdividing a floor changes air
distribution, lighting circuits, sprinkler and detector layouts, and escape
arrangements. A layout that ignores this gets revised after the ceiling is
coordinated, which is the expensive time to revise it.

Name the person who signs the layout off and the date they will do it. Treat
that date as a programme milestone.

## 4. Approvals — statutory and landlord

Two separate tracks, both with real durations.

**Statutory requirements** depend on the building, the occupancy and the layout.
Subdividing a floor can change escape arrangements and detection requirements
under **NBC 2016, Part 4**. What applies to your project, what documents are
needed and how long it takes must be established by your consultants and the
authority having jurisdiction — but it belongs on the programme from day one.

**Landlord consent** is usually required under the lease, and most buildings
have fit-out guidelines: working hours, how services may be connected, loading
dock access, protection of common areas, insurance requirements, and what must
be reinstated at lease end. Read them before design, because they constrain the
design.

## 5. Procurement and long-lead items

Items made to order — glazed partition systems, bespoke joinery, specified light
fittings, HVAC terminal equipment, switchgear — govern the programme far more
than anything built on site.

On a programme-critical project, release those packages as soon as the
information they depend on is settled, rather than waiting for the whole design
to complete. The trade-off is real and should be taken deliberately: an item
released early is expensive to change.

## 6. Site works and the services sequence

Above every ceiling, trades need the same space in order: structure, main
ductwork, sprinkler pipework, cable containment, lighting, then the ceiling
itself.

Installed out of order, work gets partly undone. The prevention is a
**coordinated services drawing agreed before installation begins**, checked at
the tightest point on the floor rather than a typical one. Building services
provisions sit in **NBC 2016, Part 8**.

This is where most of the middle of a programme is won or lost.

## 7. Commissioning

Commissioning proves the systems deliver what was designed — air balanced room
by room against design figures, controls behaving as intended, interfaces
between systems tested, fresh air quantities measured rather than assumed.

It is invisible, it sits at the end of the programme, and it is therefore the
first thing compressed when dates slip. The consequences appear after occupation
as comfort complaints, when the cause is arguable.

Ask for **measured readings against design values**, not a signed statement.

## 8. Handover

Handover is keys plus the ability to operate the building:

- As-built drawings, in editable format as well as PDF
- Operating and maintenance manuals
- Test and commissioning records
- Warranties, with start dates
- Equipment schedules and spare parts
- Training for the facilities team

Practical completion is a contractual state — the works usable for their purpose
with only minor items outstanding. It starts the defects liability period, and
disputes cluster around it, so agree the completion criteria in writing early.

## What to decide before appointing anyone

- Headcount and the growth assumption
- How many enclosed rooms, and which conversations genuinely need privacy
- Who signs the layout off, and when
- Whether the design is settled enough for a lump-sum contract, or whether
  item rates suit better
- Whether the floor will be occupied during the works
- What the lease requires you to reinstate at the end

Answer those and most of the fit-out's risk is already managed.

## Standards referenced

Fire and life safety provisions in **NBC 2016, Part 4**; building services in
**NBC 2016, Part 8**; energy performance requirements in **ECBC 2017**; interior
illumination practice in **IS 3646**. Statutory requirements, approvals, system
sizing and compliance for a specific project must be established by the
project's consultants and the authority having jurisdiction.
