    answer: "Because the loads are fundamentally different from what an ordinary shed frame carries. A crane applies moving vertical loads through the gantry, lateral surge from acceleration and braking of the trolley, and longitudinal forces from the crane travelling. Columns, gantry supports and bracing all have to resist those.   - question: "What is duty class and why does it matter?"
    answer: "Duty class describes how hard the crane works — how often it lifts, how close to capacity, and how many cycles over its life. Two cranes of identical capacity can have very different duty classes, and the class affects fatigue design of the gantry and its supports as well as the crane itself. Stating capacity without duty is an incomplete brief, and the class must come from how the plant will actually operate."
  - question: "What determines the building height?"
    answer: "Working backwards from the hook. ## The crane designs the building

On a building with an overhead crane, the crane is not equipment installed into a
shed. It is the thing the shed is built around.

Work backwards from the lift:

| Working backwards | Determines |
| --- | --- |
| Required clear lift above the floor | Minimum hook height |
| Hook, block and trolley dimensions | Gantry rail level |
| Crane bridge depth and clearances | Eaves height |
| Capacity and duty class | Gantry girder, columns, bracing, foundations |
| Span and travel length | Column spacing and building length |

Every one of those is fixed at design stage and effectively unchangeable
afterwards.

## Three load types, not one

An ordinary industrial frame carries gravity and wind. A crane building carries
those plus:

- **Moving vertical loads** through the gantry, which shift as the crane travels
  and as the trolley crosses
- **Lateral surge** from accelerating and braking the trolley, applied at rail
  level
- **Longitudinal forces** from the crane itself starting and stopping
- **Fatigue effects** from repeated cycles, which is why duty class matters

Design follows **IS 800:2007** for the steelwork with **NBC 2016, Part 6** for
structural provisions, and crane-specific requirements in **IS 807** and
**IS 3177**. The load values and duty class must come from the crane supplier and
be confirmed by the project's structural engineer.

## Duty class is part of the brief

Capacity alone is an incomplete specification.

A crane lifting near capacity many times a day is a different structural problem
from one lifting occasionally, even at the same rated load, because fatigue
governs the gantry and its connections.

The brief should state how the plant will actually operate — lifts per shift,
typical load as a proportion of capacity, and expected life — not just the
tonnage.

## Tolerances are tighter than general steel

Gantry rails have limits on alignment, level, span between rails and
straightness. A crane running on rails outside those limits:

- Wears wheels and rails quickly
- Skews as it travels, increasing lateral loads
- In severe cases, derails

These must be **surveyed and recorded during installation**, and re-checked
periodically in service. A gantry that was correct on day one does not
necessarily stay correct after years of loading and any settlement.

## What else the crane constrains

- **Services routing** — nothing may obstruct crane travel or trolley
  cross-travel, which removes a large volume from the services zone
- **Lighting** — fittings must sit clear of the crane path and still be
  maintainable
- **Floor design**, where mobile equipment shares the space
- **Foundations**, because column loads rise
- **Maintenance access** to the crane at gantry level, safely

## If a crane is even possible in future

State it at design stage. Designing the frame for a future crane costs far less
than strengthening later, and the decision can be recorded without installing
anything.

The alternative — discovering two years in that the frame cannot take the crane
operations now need — is one of the more expensive outcomes in industrial
building.

## Standards referenced

Steel construction to **IS 800:2007**; structural provisions in
**NBC 2016, Part 6**; crane design, erection and testing in **IS 807**; electric
overhead travelling cranes in **IS 3177**. Crane loads, duty class, tolerance
requirements and the resulting structural design for a specific building must be
established by the crane supplier and the project's structural engineer.
