faqs:
  - question: "What makes a building pre-engineered rather than conventional steel?"
    answer: "In a PEB the frame is designed as a system and its members are tapered — built-up sections that are deeper where bending is highest and shallower where it is not — so steel goes only where the load needs it. Conventional steel uses standard rolled sections throughout. The result is usually a lighter frame, faster fabrication and quicker erection, which is why PEB dominates warehouses and factories."
  - question: "What information is needed before a PEB can be designed?"
    answer: "Building dimensions and clear height, the intended use, any crane loads and their capacity, mezzanines, the roof and wall cladding specification, and the site location — which sets the wind and seismic loading under IS 875 Part 3 and IS 1893. Crane loads in particular change the frame substantially and have to be known at the start, not added later."
  - question: "Can a PEB be extended later?"
    answer: "Yes, and planning for it is inexpensive if done at design stage. An end wall designed as a future extension point, with the frame and foundations sized accordingly, lets the building grow by adding bays. Retrofitting that capacity into a frame designed without it is far more expensive."

## What we deliver

**Design and engineering for pre-engineered buildings** — load assessment,
frame design, bracing, secondary members, connections and fabrication drawings —
as the first stage of a PEB delivered from design to erection.

A pre-engineered building is decided almost entirely on the drawing board. Once
members are fabricated, the frame is fixed. That makes the design stage the
cheapest place to get clear span, crane capacity, future extension and cladding
right.

## Scope of works

| Stage | What we deliver |
| --- | --- |
| Requirement | Dimensions, clear height, use, cranes, mezzanines |
| Load assessment | Dead, imposed, wind and seismic loads per IS codes |
| Frame design | Primary frames with tapered members, spacing, bracing |
| Secondary members | Purlins, girts, sag rods, eave struts |
| Connections | Bolted moment and shear connections, base plates |
| Foundation loads | Reactions issued for civil foundation design |
| Drawings | General arrangement, anchor bolt plan, fabrication details |

## What drives the design

Clear span and eave height · bay spacing · crane type and capacity · mezzanine
and roof-mounted equipment loads · wind and seismic loading for the site · roof
slope and cladding type · future extension provision.

## Where projects go wrong

- **Crane loads confirmed late.** They change the frame, the columns and the
  foundations. Adding them after design is a redesign.
- **Foundation design done separately from the frame.** Anchor bolts that do not
  match the base plates stop erection on day one.
- **No provision for extension.** Cheap at design stage, expensive afterwards.
- **Roof-mounted equipment ignored.** Solar arrays, HVAC units and water tanks
  add loads the frame must carry.
- **Site loads copied from another project.** Wind and seismic loading depend on
  the location and have to be established for each site.
