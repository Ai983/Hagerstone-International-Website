  - question: "What is the difference between U-value and R-value?"
    answer: "They describe the same property from opposite directions. R-value is thermal resistance — how well a material resists heat flow, so higher is better. U-value is transmittance — how readily heat passes, so lower is better. One is the reciprocal of the other. Insulation products are usually quoted in R-value or thermal conductivity, while whole assemblies such as walls, roofs and windows are usually quoted in U-value."
  - question: "Is a low U-value what matters most in India?"
    answer: "Not usually, for glazing. In a cooling-dominated climate the dominant heat gain through a window is solar radiation, which U-value does not describe at all — that is SHGC. U-value remains important for opaque walls and roofs, and for keeping the inner surface of glazing near room temperature so it does not become a condensation surface. Both numbers matter; they are just answering different questions."
  - question: "Why does the whole-assembly U-value differ from the product's?"
    answer: "Because the assembly includes everything the product does not — frames, fixings, structural members, junctions and any gap in the insulation. These form thermal bridges that conduct heat around the insulated area. A wall with excellent insulation and poorly detailed junctions can have a whole-assembly figure noticeably worse than the insulation suggests, which is why compliance is assessed on the assembly."
  - question: "How is a window's U-value worked out?"
    answer: "It combines the glass, the frame and the edge of the glazed unit, each weighted by its area. That is why frame material and the spacer at the glass edge both affect the result, and why a window cannot simply inherit the U-value of its glass. Calculation methods for windows are set out in standards such as ISO 10077."

## Why it matters on a project

U-value is the number that appears in envelope compliance documents, and it is
routinely misapplied in two ways: quoted for a product when the requirement is
for an assembly, and treated as the only thermal number when for glazing it is
usually the less important one.

Getting it right matters because the envelope is built once. An element that
underperforms is corrected by replacing it, not by adjusting it.

## What U-value does and does not cover

| Covers | Does not cover |
| --- | --- |
| Conducted heat through the element | Solar radiation through glazing (see SHGC) |
| The combined effect of layers in an assembly | Air leakage through gaps and joints |
| Frame, glass and edge effects in a window | Ventilation heat exchange |
| Thermal bridging, when assessed on the assembly | Occupant and equipment heat gains |

## Where the number goes wrong in practice

**Thermal bridges.** Fixings, brackets, structural members and junctions conduct
heat around the insulation. On a facade with closely spaced brackets, the effect
on the assembly figure is not marginal.

**Gaps and compression.** Insulation that does not fill its space, or is
compressed to fit, does not deliver its declared value. The declared figure
assumes correct installation.

**Product substitution.** A panel or glazing unit swapped late for a
commercially similar one can change the assembly figure, and compliance was
demonstrated on the original.

Calculation methods for building components are set out in **ISO 6946**, and for
windows and doors in **ISO 10077**.

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting glass U-value as window U-value.** The frame and edge are part of the
  window and usually make it worse.
- **Comparing products on U-value alone for glazing.** SHGC generally governs
  cooling load in Indian conditions.
- **Ignoring thermal bridging.** The field of a wall is rarely where the problem
  is.
- **Accepting a substitution without rechecking.** Compliance was calculated on a
  specific build-up.
- **Forgetting air leakage.** A well-insulated envelope that leaks air performs
  worse than its U-values suggest, and leakage is not part of the U-value at all.

## Standards referenced

Envelope requirements including U-factor criteria in **ECBC 2017**; calculation
of thermal resistance and transmittance in **ISO 6946**; window and door thermal
performance in **ISO 10077**. Applicable limits and compliance for a specific
building must be established by the project's energy consultant.
