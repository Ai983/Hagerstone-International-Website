faqs:
  - question: "Is a weatherseal the same as structural silicone?"
    answer: "No. Structural silicone bonds glass to the frame and carries wind load, so it is a structural element designed by calculation and installed under controlled conditions. A weatherseal only closes the gap between panels against water and air. The two are different products with different specifications and must not be substituted for each other."
  - question: "Why does joint width matter so much?"
    answer: "Because a sealant can only stretch and compress by a limited percentage of its width, set by its movement class. A joint that is too narrow for the expected movement asks the sealant to strain beyond what it can take, and it tears or debonds. Joint width is therefore calculated from the expected thermal and structural movement and the sealant's rated movement capability, not chosen by eye."
  - question: "What does the backer rod do?"
    answer: "It controls the depth and shape of the sealant bead and stops the sealant sticking to the back of the joint. A sealant bonded on three sides cannot stretch freely and fails early. The backer rod gives two-sided adhesion and an hourglass profile, which is what lets the bead accommodate movement."
  - question: "Why do weatherseal joints fail?"
    answer: "Most failures are adhesion failures caused by preparation, not by the sealant itself: dirty or wet surfaces, a skipped primer where the manufacturer requires one, or sealing in conditions outside the product's application range. The rest come from joints designed too narrow, three-sided adhesion, or incompatible materials in contact with the sealant."
  - question: "How long does a weatherseal last?"
    answer: "It depends on the product, UV exposure, movement and workmanship, and manufacturers state their own expectations. What is certain is that it needs inspection as part of facade maintenance, because a failed weatherseal is often the first point of water entry, and replacing a bead is far cheaper than repairing the damage behind it."

## Why it matters on a project

The weatherseal is the cheapest line on the facade package and one of the most
common sources of leaks. It is the last trade on the elevation, often applied from
a cradle against a programme deadline, and every defect in it is a potential water
path.

## Weatherseal versus structural silicone

| | Weatherseal | Structural silicone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job | Keeps water and air out | Bonds glass to frame, carries wind load |
| Location | Between panels, on the outside | Between glass and frame, usually behind the face |
| Design basis | Joint movement | Structural calculation |
| Installed | Usually on site | Usually factory, under controlled conditions |
| If it fails | Leak | Safety issue |

## What makes a joint work

- **The right width.** Calculated from expected movement and the sealant's movement
  class. Sealant classification is covered by **ASTM C920** and **ISO 11600**.
- **The right depth.** Controlled by a backer rod so the bead is not too thick to
  stretch or too thin to hold.
- **Two-sided adhesion.** A bond breaker or backer rod stops the sealant sticking to
  the back of the joint.
- **Clean, dry, primed surfaces**, prepared exactly as the manufacturer specifies.
- **Compatible materials.** Gaskets, setting blocks and coatings that touch the
  sealant must be compatible with it, or they can stain or break the bond.

Practical guidance on joint design and installation is set out in **ASTM C1193**.

## Common mistakes

- **Sealing over dirt, dust or moisture**, especially on a cradle late in the day.
- **Skipping the primer** where the manufacturer requires it.
- **Three-sided adhesion** from a missing backer rod.
- **Narrow joints** set by the frame layout rather than by movement.
- **Mixing sealants from different suppliers** on the same facade without compatibility checks.
- **No adhesion test** on site before the main application starts.

## Standards referenced

Sealant specification under **ASTM C920** and **ISO 11600**; joint design and
application guidance in **ASTM C1193**. Product selection, joint sizes and
preparation for a specific facade must follow the project's facade engineer and the
sealant manufacturer's written requirements.
