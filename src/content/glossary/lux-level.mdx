    answer: "Not necessarily, and this is the central point.   - question: "What is uniformity and why does it matter?"
    answer: "Uniformity describes how evenly light is distributed across the working area, usually as the ratio of the minimum to the average. Poor uniformity means the eye constantly readjusts as people move or look across the space, which is tiring even when the average figure is correct. Widely spaced fittings chosen to hit an average cheaply are the usual cause."
  - question: "What about glare?"
    answer: "Glare is discomfort or loss of visibility caused by excessive contrast, typically from a bright fitting in the field of view or a reflection on a screen. It is assessed separately from illuminance, and a space can be simultaneously well lit by the numbers and uncomfortable to sit in. Fitting selection, mounting position and shielding control it, not the lux figure."
  - question: "Does the target depend on the task?"
    answer: "Yes. What applies to a specific space must be established by the project's lighting consultant rather than applied as one number across a whole floor."

## Why it matters on a project

Lighting is usually specified with a single number, and that number is lux.
It is the easiest thing to measure, the easiest to write into a brief and the
easiest to verify with a handheld meter on handover day.

It is also the reason offices get signed off as compliant and still feel badly
lit.

## What lux does not tell you

| Criterion | What it covers | Described by lux? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Illuminance | How much light lands on the surface | Yes |
| Uniformity | How evenly it is distributed | No |
| Glare | Discomfort from contrast and reflections | No |
| Colour temperature | Whether light reads warm or cool | No |
| Colour rendering | Whether finishes look right under it | No |
| Control | Whether output responds to daylight and occupancy | No |

A specification stating only a lux figure has left five of those six to chance.

## The uniformity trap

The cheapest way to hit an average illuminance is fewer, brighter fittings
spaced further apart.

The meter confirms the average. The occupants experience bright pools under each
fitting and dim zones between them, and the eye adapts continuously as they move
or turn. It is a measurable pass and a poor room.

Specifying uniformity alongside illuminance closes this, and it costs nothing at
design stage.

## Colour is not a detail

Two aspects matter and both are invisible on a lux meter:

- **Colour temperature** sets whether the space reads warm or cool. Mixing
  temperatures across one floor — often the result of replacing failed fittings
  later with whatever is available — looks conspicuously wrong.
- **Colour rendering** determines whether finishes, materials and skin tones look
  natural. It matters most where the interior was chosen for its materials,
  which is to say in exactly the spaces a client cares about.

Recommended values by task are set out in **IS 3646** and **EN 12464-1**, with
power and control requirements in **ECBC 2017**.

## Common mistakes

- **Specifying lux alone.** Five other criteria are then unmanaged.
- **Applying one figure across a whole floor.** Tasks differ; requirements differ.
- **Ignoring uniformity.** The cheapest way to pass is the worst way to live.
- **Forgetting screens.** Reflected glare on monitors is the most common lighting
  complaint in offices and is a positioning problem.
- **Mixing colour temperatures over time.** Replacement fittings should match the
  original specification, which means recording it.
- **No daylight-linked control.** A well-daylit perimeter running at full output
  wastes the daylight and the energy.

## Standards referenced

Interior illumination practice in **IS 3646**; workplace lighting in
**EN 12464-1**; lighting power and control requirements in **ECBC 2017**.
Design illuminance, uniformity and glare criteria for a specific space must be
established by the project's lighting consultant.
