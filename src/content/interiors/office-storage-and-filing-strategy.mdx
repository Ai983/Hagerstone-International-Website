## Unplanned storage still happens

The decision is not whether to have storage. It is whether it is designed or
improvised.

An office that does not plan it ends up with cabinets against every spare wall,
boxes beneath desks, and cupboards in circulation — consuming area that nobody
allocated, looking untidy, and sometimes obstructing escape routes.

## Audit before you plan

The audit is more useful than any allowance, and it produces a consistent
finding: **most stored material is rarely accessed.**

| Category | Typical volume | Access frequency | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Active working files | Small | Daily | Near the team |
| Team reference | Moderate | Occasional | Shared, on the floor |
| Statutory records | Fixed by requirement | Rare | Archive or off-site |
| Historic and superseded | Often the largest | Almost never | Disposal or off-site |
| Personal belongings | Per person | Daily | Pedestal or locker |
| Supplies and consumables | Moderate | Regular | Near the pantry or print area |

A move is the only realistic opportunity a business gets to dispose of what it
no longer needs. Auditing before the layout is drawn captures that opportunity;
auditing afterwards just relocates the problem.

## Personal storage follows the working pattern

With **assigned desks**, pedestals work and are the obvious answer.

With **shared or hybrid desks**, pedestals cannot work — there is no permanent
desk to attach them to. Lockers are the alternative, sized for peak attendance
rather than total headcount, and positioned where people pass on arrival rather
than tucked away.

Getting this wrong is immediately visible. Too few lockers and personal items
accumulate on desks and windowsills; too many and they occupy area that could
have been useful.

## Statutory retention sets a floor

Some records must be retained for defined periods regardless of how rarely they
are consulted. Those requirements should be established with the business — they
are specific to the organisation and its obligations — and the resulting volume
planned for deliberately.

The right answer is usually archive or off-site storage rather than prime floor
area, but it has to be an answer rather than an oversight.

## Two technical considerations

**Floor loading.** Dense paper storage is heavy, and mobile shelving systems
concentrate substantial load on a small footprint. Whether a floor can take it
is a structural question under **NBC 2016, Part 6**, to be confirmed by the
project's structural engineer before such a system is specified.

**Fire and escape.** Stored material contributes to fire load, and storage must
not obstruct escape routes or access to fire equipment. What applies to a
specific floor is determined under **NBC 2016, Part 4** by the fire consultant
and the authority having jurisdiction.

## Make storage do second jobs

Storage that only stores is consuming area. Storage walls can simultaneously:

- Provide **acoustic separation** between zones
- **Conceal services**, columns or an irregular building edge
- Define circulation without a full-height partition
- Carry **wayfinding** or team identification

Designed this way, storage stops being an overhead and becomes part of how the
floor works.

## Standards referenced

Fire and life safety provisions, including escape routes and fire load, in
**NBC 2016, Part 4**; structural provisions including imposed loads in
**NBC 2016, Part 6**. Floor loading capacity, fire load assessment and escape
arrangements for a specific floor must be established by the project's
structural engineer and fire consultant and the authority having jurisdiction.
