## Wayfinding starts with routes, not walls

The common approach is to complete a fit-out, notice people getting lost, and
add signs where they hesitate.

The designed approach is to walk the routes first:

- A **visitor** arriving at the building entrance, reaching reception, then a
  meeting room, then finding the toilets
- A **new employee** finding their desk, the pantry, the fire exits, the meeting
  rooms
- A **delivery** reaching the correct door without crossing the visitor route

At every point where one of those journeys requires a choice, information is
needed. Between those points, it is not.

## The hierarchy

| Level | Answers | Placed at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Which floor, which zone? | Lift lobbies, main entrances |
| Secondary | Which way from here? | Route junctions, corridor decisions |
| Tertiary | What is this room? | At each door or room |
| Statutory | Where is the exit? | As required, independent of the above |

Keeping the levels distinct is what makes a system readable. A sign trying to
serve two levels at once usually serves neither.

## Statutory signage is not a design decision

Exit signs, escape route marking, assembly points and safety notices are
requirements. Their positions, sizes, visibility and illumination — including
behaviour during a power failure — are governed by the applicable rules for the
building.

What applies to a specific project is determined under **NBC 2016, Part 4** and
local requirements by the fire consultant and the authority having jurisdiction.
Fire safety signs are addressed in **IS 12349**, with graphical symbols in
**ISO 7010**.

The practical design point: statutory signage must not compete visually with
wayfinding or branding. If a bold feature sign draws attention away from an exit
sign, the design has created a hazard.

## Why it belongs in the design, not the snag list

Signage needs things that have to exist before the walls are finished:

- **Power** for illuminated signs, without surface cabling
- **Fixings** suited to the construction behind — glass, plasterboard, stone and
  timber all differ
- **Lighting coordination**, so signs are legible rather than in shadow, with
  interior illumination practice in **IS 3646**
- **Wall finishes** that the sign can sit on cleanly
- **Space**, so a sign is not competing with a switch plate or a fire panel

Every one of those is straightforward at design stage and awkward afterwards.

## Materials and durability

Signs live at hand height on routes people use daily:

- Edges and corners get knocked
- Surfaces get touched and cleaned repeatedly
- Applied vinyl lifts at the edges over time
- Sunlit positions fade

Specify for that, and keep the system **extensible** — a floor will be
reconfigured, rooms will be renamed, and a signage system that cannot absorb
change gets replaced piecemeal with mismatched items.

## Room numbering

Adopt a logical scheme at the outset — floor, zone, sequence — record it, and
leave gaps for future rooms. Numbering invented during the fit-out produces a
building nobody can navigate and a facilities record nobody can use.

## Standards referenced

Fire and life safety provisions, including exit signage, in
**NBC 2016, Part 4**; fire safety signs in **IS 12349**; graphical symbols for
safety signs in **ISO 7010**; interior illumination practice in **IS 3646**.
Statutory signage requirements for a specific building must be determined by the
project's fire consultant and the authority having jurisdiction.
