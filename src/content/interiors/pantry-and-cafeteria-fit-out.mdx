## Find the stacks before drawing the plan

The usual process is backwards: the layout is designed, a pantry is placed where
it suits the plan, and the services team is then asked to serve it.

The order that works is to establish where existing soil and waste stacks are,
where extract can be routed, and where water and power can reasonably reach —
and to place wet areas accordingly. Everything else on a floor is more flexible
than drainage.

## The four services, in order of constraint

| Service | Why it constrains | Flexibility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Drainage** | Gravity; needs fall to an existing stack | Very low |
| **Extract** | Must route through the building and discharge | Low |
| **Water supply** | Pressurised, routes reasonably freely | Moderate |
| **Power** | Concentrated load; needs a dedicated circuit | Moderate |

Drainage is almost always the binding constraint. Floor build-up available for a
fall is limited, and a long run either runs out of fall or needs pumping — which
introduces equipment, noise, maintenance and something that can fail while
nobody is watching.

Plumbing provisions sit in **NBC 2016, Part 9**, with ventilation in
**NBC 2016, Part 8** and ventilation rates commonly referenced to
**ASHRAE 62.1**.

## Name what you are actually building

| | Tea point | Pantry | Cafeteria with cooking |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Water and sink drainage | Yes | Yes | Yes, plus floor drainage |
| Refrigeration | Sometimes | Yes | Yes, larger |
| Extract | Minimal | Moderate | Substantial, with grease management |
| Power | Modest | Concentrated | Heavy, or gas |
| Back of house | None | Small | Preparation, storage, wash-up, waste |
| Fire considerations | Standard | Standard | Suppression and dampers likely |

Briefs frequently say "pantry" and mean "cafeteria". The services difference is
large, and it is better established at brief stage than during coordination.

## What consumes the area

A cafeteria brief given as a seat count understates the space required. Also
needed:

- Servery and queuing space, which backs up at peak
- Circulation between tables that people can actually pass through
- Back-of-house preparation and storage
- Wash-up and equipment space
- Waste storage and its removal route
- Sometimes a separate route for deliveries

And the sizing driver is **peak usage**, not headcount — a single lunch sitting
needs far more seats than a staggered one, so the operating pattern belongs in
the brief.

## Construction details that matter

- **Impervious, slip-resistant flooring**, with coved junctions at walls in wet
  areas
- **Falls to drainage** designed into the screed wherever the floor is washed
- **Waterproofing** beneath wet areas, properly detailed at upstands
- **Ventilation** sized for the space's actual use, not the general office rate
- **Acoustic treatment** — hard, cleanable surfaces make these rooms loud, and a
  noisy cafeteria empties quickly
- **Access** to isolation valves, traps and equipment for maintenance

## Standards referenced

Plumbing provisions, including water supply and drainage, in
**NBC 2016, Part 9**; ventilation provisions in **NBC 2016, Part 8**;
ventilation rates commonly referenced to **ASHRAE 62.1**; fire and life safety
provisions in **NBC 2016, Part 4**. Drainage design, extract routing,
ventilation rates and any fire requirements for a specific facility must be
established by the project's consultants and the authority having jurisdiction.
