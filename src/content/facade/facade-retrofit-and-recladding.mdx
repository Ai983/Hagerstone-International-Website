## Investigation comes before design

On a new building the facade engineer starts from the architect's intent. On a
retrofit they start from what is already there — and what is already there is
rarely what the drawings say.

| Investigate | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Substrate type and condition | Determines what can be fixed to it |
| Existing fixings | Reusable, or must be replaced entirely? |
| Insulation present and its state | Wet insulation indicates a water path |
| Structural capacity | Can it take the new system's loads? |
| Existing water paths and defects | The cause, not just the symptom |
| Legacy materials | Some require particular handling |

Opening up at representative locations is the single highest-value early spend
on a retrofit. Every rupee saved by skipping it tends to return as a variation.

## Overclad or replace

**Overcladding** — adding a new outer layer over the existing — avoids opening
the interior, is faster, and is less disruptive to occupants. But it adds load,
increases wall thickness which affects reveals, sills and roof edges, and leaves
whatever is underneath in place.

**Replacement** removes the existing envelope entirely. It is more disruptive
and more expensive, but it addresses the actual condition and allows the new
build-up to be designed properly rather than around an unknown.

The deciding question is usually: **is the existing envelope merely tired, or is
it failing?** Building over a failing layer preserves the problem.

## Loads have to be re-established

A new facade transfers load through its fixings into the existing structure.
Design wind pressure comes from **IS 875 (Part 3)** for the building's actual
site, height and terrain — and may differ from what the original design assumed,
particularly if surroundings have changed.

Whether the substrate can accept those loads is an assessment, normally
including **pull-out testing of trial fixings** in the actual substrate. Those
values are the structural and facade engineers' output, not something to carry
over from the original construction.

## Keeping the building dry and in use

- Work in **controlled zones**, with temporary weather protection
- Sequence so no area is open longer than necessary
- Plan around **monsoon conditions** rather than hoping
- Maintain the interior environment — cooling, or its absence, in opened zones
- Protect interiors even where work appears external

A partly removed facade during heavy rain damages interiors at a cost far
exceeding careful sequencing.

## Requirements for an altered building

What an existing building must achieve when altered depends on the extent of
the work, its classification and the applicable rules — including envelope
performance under **ECBC 2017** and fire requirements under
**NBC 2016, Part 4**.

This has to be established at the outset by the project's consultants and the
authority having jurisdiction, because it determines what the new envelope must
deliver and therefore what it will cost. Establishing it late is how a retrofit
budget doubles.

## Occupants and access

Working at height on an occupied building brings safety requirements addressed
in **NBC 2016, Part 7**, plus practical matters: access equipment outside
windows, exclusion zones below, agreed working hours, and clear communication to
occupants about what happens to their area and when.

## Standards referenced

Wind loading to **IS 875 (Part 3)**; envelope performance in **ECBC 2017**;
fire and life safety in **NBC 2016, Part 4**; construction practices and safety
in **NBC 2016, Part 7**. Structural capacity, fixing design, applicable
performance requirements and safety arrangements for a specific retrofit must be
established by the project's facade engineer, structural engineer and fire
consultant, and confirmed with the authority having jurisdiction.
