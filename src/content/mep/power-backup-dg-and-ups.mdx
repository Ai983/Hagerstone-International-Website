## Start with the load, not the plant

The design sequence that works:

1. **Define the essential load** — what must run, and for how long
2. **Segregate it** at the distribution boards
3. **Size the UPS** to bridge the transfer gap for loads that cannot be
   interrupted
4. **Size the generator** for the essential load plus realistic growth
5. **Resolve siting** — space, fuel, exhaust, ventilation, noise
6. **Plan the testing regime** before handover

Starting at step four, which is common, produces equipment chosen against a
number nobody has justified.

## The two cover different windows

| | UPS | DG set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transfer | Instantaneous | Start-up delay |
| Duration | Limited by batteries | As long as fuel lasts |
| Typical loads | IT, network, controls | Lifts, HVAC, lighting, power |
| Role | Bridges the gap | Sustains the building |

A building with only a generator loses everything at the moment of failure. A
building with only a UPS keeps running briefly and then stops. Critical
installations use both in series.

## Defining the essential load is the real work

If everything is essential, the generator is oversized and expensive. If the
definition is too narrow, something important is discovered missing during the
first real outage.

The list is business-specific and should be made deliberately:

- Servers, network and communications
- Security, access control, CCTV
- Emergency lighting and escape route lighting
- Lifts, as applicable
- Cooling for server rooms and any temperature-critical space
- Process or storage that cannot be interrupted
- A sensible share of general lighting and power for continued working

## Segregation must be physical

A notional distinction does not survive the first office churn.

Essential circuits come from boards fed by the backup supply. UPS-fed outlets
are identifiable, commonly by socket colour, so a kettle does not end up on the
server supply. Both should be recorded on the as-built drawings and in the
handover documentation.

Installation practice follows **IS 732** and the building services provisions of
**NBC 2016, Part 8**, with switchgear selection addressed in **IS 10118**.
Circuit design and sizing are the electrical consultant's output.

## Siting and compliance

Generators bring physical and regulatory constraints:

- **Space and access** for the set, and for maintenance
- **Fuel storage**, with its own requirements
- **Exhaust routing**, discharging safely away from air intakes and occupied
  areas
- **Ventilation** for combustion and cooling air
- **Vibration isolation** from the structure
- **Noise and emissions**, administered through **CPCB norms** in India, which
  commonly drive acoustic enclosure and exhaust design

What applies to a specific installation, and any consent obligations, must be
confirmed with the electrical consultant and the relevant authority.

## Both fail silently

This is the point worth carrying into operations. Neither system announces its
own degradation.

Periodic testing **under actual load**, with recorded results, is the only way to
know. Agree the regime and its owner before handover, or the capital investment
becomes a maintenance item nobody holds.

## Standards referenced

Electrical installation practice in **IS 732**; building services provisions in
**NBC 2016, Part 8**; switchgear selection and installation in **IS 10118**;
diesel generator emission and noise requirements administered through
**CPCB norms**. Load assessment, equipment sizing, protection design and
compliance obligations for a specific building must be established by the
project's electrical consultant and confirmed with the relevant authority.
