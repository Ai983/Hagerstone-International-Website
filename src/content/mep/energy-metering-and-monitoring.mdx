## Measurement is the prerequisite

You cannot manage consumption you cannot see. A single incoming meter produces
one number a month, which tells you what you spent and nothing about why.

Sub-metering divides that number into parts a facilities team can investigate
and act on.

## What to meter

| Category | Why it is worth separating |
| --- | --- |
| HVAC plant | Usually the largest single consumer in a commercial building |
| Lighting | Reveals out-of-hours running and control failures |
| Small power | Tracks occupancy-related consumption and equipment growth |
| Data centre or server room | Continuous, dense, and often larger than assumed |
| Kitchen or cafeteria | Concentrated and intermittent |
| Process equipment | On industrial sites, frequently dominant |
| By floor or tenancy | Essential where consumption is recharged |

The test for whether to meter something: **would a change in this reading point
to something specific you could do?** If not, the meter adds cost without
information.

## The first finding is almost always out-of-hours

When a building is metered properly for the first time, the most common
discovery is how much it consumes when nobody is in it.

Plant running on schedules that no longer match occupancy, lighting circuits
left on, equipment never switched off, and systems that were put into manual
during a complaint and never returned — these accumulate quietly and appear only
when consumption can be seen against time.

It is usually the largest available saving, and it costs almost nothing to fix.

## Metering without review is a cost, not a saving

This is the point worth making before specifying anything.

Meters do not reduce consumption. A person looking at the data and acting on it
does. A building with comprehensive metering and nobody reviewing it has bought
instrumentation and received no return.

So the design question is not only "what shall we meter" but **"who reviews
this, how often, and what are they empowered to change?"** If the answer is
nobody, a simpler installation is the honest choice.

Energy management systems are addressed by **ISO 50001**, and monitoring and
metering provisions for commercial buildings by **ECBC 2017**.

## Practical design points

- **Meter where circuits are already segregated** — metering is far easier if the
  distribution design anticipated it
- **Half-hourly or better resolution** where consumption against time matters,
  which is what reveals out-of-hours running
- **Communications** planned — meters need a route to wherever data is collected
- **Labelling and documentation**, so readings can be attributed years later
- **Integration with the BMS** where correlation between consumption and plant
  behaviour is wanted, specified deliberately

Installation follows **IS 732** and the building services provisions of
**NBC 2016, Part 8**, and the metering scheme should be set by the project's
electrical and energy consultants.

## A workable starting point

1. Decide who will review the data and how often — before designing anything
2. Meter the major categories: HVAC, lighting, small power, large individual
   loads
3. Ensure resolution is fine enough to show consumption against time
4. Label and document the scheme for the building's future
5. Review the first months deliberately, looking first at out-of-hours
6. Extend metering only where a finer breakdown would change a decision

## Standards referenced

Energy monitoring and metering provisions in **ECBC 2017**; energy management
systems in **ISO 50001**; electrical installation practice in **IS 732**;
building services provisions in **NBC 2016, Part 8**. The metering requirements
applying to a specific building must be determined by the project's energy and
electrical consultants.
