## Handover is not the day you get the keys

For most clients, handover means walking the floor, signing a completion note
and receiving keys. The paperwork is treated as a formality that follows.

It is not a formality. **The documents are what you use in year two**, when the
air conditioning fails on one side of the floor, a cable needs to be added, a
warranty claim is disputed, or a refit needs to know what is behind a wall.
On that day, the person solving the problem was probably not on the project and
the people who were may have moved on.

For a CFO, the documents are the evidence that supports every future claim. For
a facilities head, they are the manual for the space. Both are much cheaper to
agree before the contract is signed than to chase after completion.

## Fit-out handover documents: what you should receive

| Document | Why it matters | Who needs it later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| As-built drawings | Show what was installed, not what was intended | Facilities, next contractor |
| O&M manuals | How to run and maintain each system | Facilities, maintenance contractor |
| Test and commissioning records | Show each system was tested and set up | Facilities, consultants, insurers |
| Warranties, dates, serial numbers | Set what is covered, until when | Finance, facilities |
| Material and finish schedules | Identify products so they can be matched or replaced | Facilities, designers |
| Fire and electrical system records | Show what protects the space and how it is arranged | Facilities, safety officer, authority |
| Keys and access schedule | Record who holds access to what | Facilities, security |
| Facilities team training | Lets your team operate what was installed | Facilities |

Not every project needs every document, and the list should be adapted to the
scope. The point is to **decide it before work starts**.

## Why as-builts are not the design drawings

Design drawings describe the intent. Site changes the reality.

Routes shift around a beam. A duct moves because a sprinkler main is in the way.
A substitution is approved because a specified item is unavailable. A
dimension changes because the wall was not where the survey said. Each change is
reasonable, and none of them appears on the original drawing.

The difference is greatest **above the ceiling and inside walls**, the places
that cannot be checked by looking. That is exactly where a future contractor
needs the truth, because they are about to cut into it.

Two practical points:

- **Ask for as-builts to be updated progressively**, not reconstructed at the
  end from memory. A drawing revised during the works is more accurate than one
  reconstructed afterwards.
- **Ask what the as-builts are based on.** Marked-up site drawings, a survey or
  a full redraw are different levels of reliability, and the contract can
  say which is required.

## Test and commissioning records are the proof

A system that looks finished may not be working properly. Commissioning is the
process of testing and setting up each system, and the record of it is your
evidence that the work was done.

For services, that means records for the air-conditioning and ventilation,
electrical installation, fire detection and firefighting systems, and
data networks, in the form each system needs. The provisions for building
services sit in **NBC 2016, Part 8**, and electrical wiring practice in
**IS 732**. Earthing arrangements are part of the electrical picture; see
[earthing and bonding](/glossary/earthing-and-bonding).

What tests are required, and what results are acceptable, are set by the
project's engineers and the specifications. The client's job is to make sure the
records exist, are signed, and are handed over, not to judge the values.

## Warranties: the dates are the point

A warranty is only as useful as the record behind it. Three details decide
whether a claim works:

1. **The start date.** Warranties may run from delivery, installation,
   commissioning or handover, depending on their terms. Equipment delivered long
   before handover may already have used part of its cover.
2. **The serial number.** Without it, matching a failed unit to a warranty is a
   dispute.
3. **The scope and conditions.** Some warranties depend on servicing at set
   intervals by an approved party. Miss that and the cover may lapse.

Record all three at handover, in one register. It takes an afternoon. Rebuilding
it in year two, from delivery notes and emails, takes weeks and may not be
possible.

## The defects liability period and your records

After completion, the contract usually gives a period during which the
contractor must put right defects. Its length and terms are set by the contract
and should be read in yours.

The handover records make that period work. They establish **what was installed
and the condition it was handed over in**, so a defect can be described, dated
and evidenced. Without them, a claim depends on memory.

If the fit-out is split between the landlord's base works and your own
fit-out, the boundary matters too. Our note on
[Cat A versus Cat B fit-out](/glossary/cat-a-vs-cat-b-fit-out) explains how the
split is usually drawn. It affects which party holds records for which
elements, and it is worth agreeing at the start.

## Agree it in the contract, not at completion

The most reliable approach is to write the handover requirement into the
contract or scope document:

- **A list** of the documents required.
- **A format**, including drawing formats and how many copies.
- **A date**, ideally before or at practical completion.
- **Consequences**, such as retention or a final payment linked to delivery.
  Whether this is appropriate is a matter for your own contract.

This is the same discipline as the rest of the contract structure. Our
comparison of [turnkey and item-rate contracts](/compare/turnkey-vs-item-rate-fit-out-contract)
covers how scope is defined, and handover documentation belongs inside it. The
time needed to assemble and check the documents also belongs in the programme;
see [where fit-out time is lost](/blog/office-fit-out-programme-where-time-is-lost).

## Statutory documents are a separate question

Documents that a building or authority requires on completion or before
occupation are a separate subject from contractual handover documents. Which of
them apply to a specific project, and in what form, depends on the building, its
use and the local authority. That must be established by the project's
consultants and the authority having jurisdiction. Fire and life safety
provisions sit in **NBC 2016, Part 4**, and nothing here replaces that advice.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the document list to completion.** The result is partial sets,
  assembled in a hurry.
- **Accepting design drawings as as-builts.**
- **Not recording warranty start dates and serial numbers**, then losing the
  ability to claim.
- **Skipping training**, so nobody in the facilities team knows how the
  systems are meant to operate.
- **Receiving documents that cannot be read later**, in a format or storage
  that has moved on.
- **Not checking the handover set** against the list before signing.

## What to ask

- Which documents will we receive, in what format, and by what date?
- Will the as-builts be updated during the works or reconstructed at the end?
- Where are the test and commissioning records, and who has signed them?
- Is there a warranty register with start dates and serial numbers?
- What training will the facilities team receive?
- Which statutory documents does the project require, and who is obtaining them?

## Standards referenced

Building services provisions in **NBC 2016, Part 8**; fire and life safety in
**NBC 2016, Part 4**; electrical wiring installations under **IS 732**. The
documents required for a specific project, and any statutory completion or
occupancy documents, must be established by the project's consultants and the
authority having jurisdiction.
