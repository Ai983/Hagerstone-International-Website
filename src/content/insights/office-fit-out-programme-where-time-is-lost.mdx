## The question behind the question

When someone asks how long a fit-out takes, they are usually asking something
more specific: **can we be in by a date.** That date is normally tied to a lease
expiry, a headcount plan or a business event, and it is rarely negotiable.

The useful answer is not a duration. It is knowing which four things govern the
date, because those are the only things worth managing closely.

## The four governors

| Governor | Why it controls the date | What shortens it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design freeze | Nothing can be ordered against a moving design | A named decision-maker, available |
| Long-lead items | Manufactured to order, not held in stock | Early release of settled packages |
| Services sequence | Trades share one ceiling void, in order | Coordinated drawings before installation |
| Statutory approvals | Run on their own timetable | Starting them at design stage |

Notice what is absent: site labour. On most fit-outs, adding people to the floor
does not move the completion date, because the constraint is a decision or a
delivery rather than an hour of work.

## Design freeze is a commercial decision, not a design one

Programmes slip at the front far more often than at the back, and the cause is
usually organisational rather than technical — the layout is nearly settled, one
stakeholder has not confirmed, and the package cannot be released.

Two weeks lost here is not two weeks lost overall. It delays procurement, which
delays the item with the longest lead time, which delays the trade that follows
it. A short delay at freeze becomes a longer delay at handover.

The practical control is to **name the person who signs off the layout and the
date they will do it**, and treat that date as a programme milestone with the
same status as a delivery.

## Release long-lead packages early, even if the rest is not ready

The instinct is to procure once, when the design is complete. On a
programme-critical project that is the wrong instinct.

Items manufactured to order can be released as soon as the information they
depend on is settled, and that is usually well before the whole design is
finished. Partition systems, joinery, specified lighting and terminal equipment
frequently only need dimensions and specification — not the whole package.

The trade-off is real and should be stated plainly: **an item released early is
expensive to change.** That is a decision to take deliberately, weighing the
programme benefit against the likelihood of a change, rather than one to avoid
by default.

## The ceiling is where the middle of the programme is won or lost

Above every suspended ceiling, several trades need the same space in a
particular order — structure first, then main ductwork, then sprinkler
pipework, then cable containment, then lighting, then the ceiling.

Installed out of order, work gets partly undone. A duct routed where a sprinkler
main has to run means one of them moves, and by then both are fixed.

The prevention is a **coordinated services drawing agreed before anything is
installed**, checked at the tightest point on the floor rather than a typical
one. It takes days. Discovering the clash on site takes weeks. Building services
provisions are set out in **NBC 2016, Part 8**.

## Approvals are an activity, not paperwork

The most avoidable delays come from treating statutory requirements as something
handled near completion.

Requirements depend on the building, the occupancy and the layout — and
subdividing a floor into cabins and meeting rooms can change escape
arrangements, which is why it belongs in the conversation while the layout is
still a drawing. Fire and life safety provisions sit in **NBC 2016, Part 4**.

What applies to a specific project, what documents are needed, and how long the
process takes must be established by the project's consultants and the
authority having jurisdiction. The point here is only that it has a duration,
and that duration belongs on the programme from the start.

## What to ask before agreeing a date

- Who signs off the layout, and on what date?
- Which items have the longest lead time, and when must they be ordered?
- Has a coordinated services drawing been produced, and checked at the worst
  point?
- What approvals does this occupancy and layout require, and who is running
  them?
- Is the floor occupied during the works, and has the phasing been planned?
- What is the sequence for commissioning, snagging and handover — and is that
  time in the programme, or assumed away?

A contractor who can answer all six has a programme. One who offers a duration
without them has an estimate.

## Standards referenced

Fire and life safety provisions, including occupancy and escape, in
**NBC 2016, Part 4**; building services provisions in **NBC 2016, Part 8**.
Statutory requirements, approval processes and their durations for a specific
project must be established by the project's consultants and the authority
having jurisdiction.
