## What the decision turns on

A wall finish in a corridor, lift lobby or hotel corridor takes suitcase
wheels, trolleys, chair backs and daily cleaning. Paint in those positions is
repainted often, and every repaint means closing an area, masking and letting
it cure. **Commercial vinyl wallcovering** is the usual answer: a PVC wear
surface on a fabric or non-woven backing, hung with adhesive, that can be
scrubbed and takes impact.

The misconception is that vinyl is simply "tougher paint". It is a different
system with one serious risk: it is close to vapour-tight. On a wall that is
damp, or an external wall in a humid climate with air-conditioning inside,
moisture collects behind it and mould grows unseen until it stains through.
In a hotel that means rooms out of service; in a clinic, an infection-control
problem. The wall decides whether vinyl is safe before the finish does.

## Vinyl vs paint vs fabric

| Property | Paint | Commercial vinyl | Fabric / textile |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Impact and scuff resistance | Low | High (heavy-duty grades highest) | Low to moderate |
| Washability | Finish dependent | High; scrubbable grades | Low |
| Vapour permeability | High | Very low; breathable types available | High |
| Repair | Touch-up or repaint | Replace a drop or panel | Replace a drop |
| Hides wall defects | Poorly | Well | Well |
| Typical use | General office walls | Corridors, lobbies, hotels, clinics | Boardrooms, suites, executive areas |
| Relative cost | Lowest | Higher | Higher |

For walls that will be painted, see [low-VOC paint for offices](/materials/low-voc-paint).

## Durability classes and standards

Wallcoverings in roll form are covered in Europe by **EN 15102** for decorative
wallcoverings, **EN 233** for finished wallpapers, wall vinyls and plastics
wallcoverings, and **EN 259-1** for heavy duty wallcoverings, which sets
requirements including washability, impact resistance and colour fastness to
light. In American practice, **ASTM F793** classifies wall coverings by use
characteristics, from decorative grades to those designed for high
serviceability.

In specifications, durability is often described by weight and class: lighter
vinyls for low-traffic rooms, heavier, heavy-duty grades for corridors and
public areas. The class should be named in the specification and matched to
the product's test data, not inferred from thickness or feel.

## Fire classification

Wallcoverings are tested for reaction to fire, commonly classified under
**EN 13501-1**, with ratings for flame spread, smoke and burning droplets. The
result depends on the product **and the substrate and adhesive** it was tested
on, so a classification on non-combustible board may not apply to vinyl hung on
a timber panel. What a corridor, lobby or escape route requires is decided
under **NBC 2016 Part 4** by the fire consultant and the authority having
jurisdiction.

## Mould risk on walls with moisture

Vinyl traps moisture that would otherwise escape through the wall face. The
risk is highest on:

- **External walls** in humid regions where the room is air-conditioned, so
  moisture drives inward and meets the cool wallcovering
- **Walls with leaks or rising damp**, or behind wet areas and shafts
- **New plaster or blockwork** that has not dried out

The mechanism is the same as the one described in
[office window condensation and mould](/blog/office-window-condensation-and-mould).
Responses include fixing the moisture source first, choosing a breathable or
microperforated wallcovering, using a mould-inhibiting adhesive, and asking
the envelope or MEP consultant to review the wall build-up.

## Substrate preparation

- **Dry and sound**: plaster and blockwork dried out and checked before hanging
- **Flat**: defects filled and sanded; heavy vinyl hides minor marks, not bumps
- **Primed and sized** as the manufacturer specifies, so the adhesive bonds and
  drops can be stripped later
- **Correct adhesive** for the product weight and backing
- **Drops from one batch** hung in sequence so colour stays consistent

## Where it is used

- **Office corridors, lift lobbies and meeting rooms** that take impact
- **Hospitality**: hotel corridors and guest rooms, where downtime is costly;
  see [luxury and hospitality interiors](/services/interior-fit-out/luxury-and-hospitality-interiors)
- **Healthcare**: corridors and waiting areas, where cleanability matters and
  products must suit the cleaning regime the infection-control team uses;
  see [hospitals and healthcare](/industries/hospitals-and-healthcare)

## Common mistakes

- **Vinyl on a damp external wall**, followed by mould within a season.
- **Hung on wet plaster** to keep the programme moving.
- **Fire class taken from a test on a different substrate.**
- **Mixed batches**, so the colour shifts at a joint.
- **Light decorative grade in a heavy-traffic corridor**, torn by trolleys
  within months.
- **No spare rolls ordered** for future repairs from the same batch.

## What to ask your contractor or supplier

- Which durability class and standard is the product tested to?
- What is the reaction-to-fire classification, and on what substrate?
- Is the wall dry, and has the moisture risk been checked?
- Is a breathable or microperforated grade needed here?
- Which adhesive and primer, and how many spare rolls will be handed over?

## Standards referenced

Decorative wallcoverings under **EN 15102**, finished wallpapers and wall
vinyls under **EN 233**, heavy duty wallcoverings under **EN 259-1**, and use
classification under **ASTM F793**. Reaction-to-fire classification under
**EN 13501-1**, with building requirements under **NBC 2016 Part 4**. Product
class, fire performance and the suitability of a wall for vinyl must be
confirmed by the project's interior designer, fire consultant and envelope or
MEP consultant.
