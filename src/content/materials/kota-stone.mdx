## Why Kota stone is still specified

**Kota stone** is a fine-grained limestone from the Kota belt of Rajasthan,
usually blue-green or brown, cut into slabs and laid on a cement mortar bed. It
remains common in Indian commercial and institutional buildings because it does
two things well: it takes years of heavy foot traffic without much care, and a
worn floor can be reground and repolished rather than replaced.

The common misconception is that Kota is a "low-maintenance stone you can put
anywhere". It is a limestone. Acids etch it, oils stain it, and a polished
finish becomes slippery when wet. Most disappointment with Kota floors comes
from using one finish everywhere and cleaning it with the wrong products. The
cost of getting it wrong is not the stone; it is closing a corridor or staircase
later to regrind it, or living with stained pantry and washroom floors.

## Finishes and where each suits

| Finish | What it looks like | Grip when wet | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Natural / rough** | Split surface, matt, slightly uneven | Good | External paths, ramps, parking, terraces, wet utility areas |
| **Machine-cut, ground** | Calibrated slabs, flat, matt to low sheen | Moderate | Corridors, staircases, canteens, factory amenity areas |
| **Polished** | Smooth, reflective | Reduced | Dry lobbies and internal floors where appearance leads |
| **Honed** | Smooth, matt | Moderate | Interiors that need a flat surface without gloss |

Stair treads deserve a separate decision. A polished tread with a sharp nosing
looks clean but is where slips happen; a grooved or rough-finished nosing strip
is a common remedy.

## How it is laid and finished

Kota is laid on a **cement-sand mortar bed** over a sound, clean base. Good
practice is a full bed under every slab, not dabs, so the slab is supported
across its whole area. Slab thickness is typically in the range of about 20 to
40 mm depending on use and slab size; the supplier's data and the project
engineer confirm the thickness for heavier duties.

After the bed sets, the floor is usually **ground in situ** to remove lipping
between slabs and then polished to the specified finish. This is wet work that
produces slurry, so it has to be sequenced before skirtings, joinery and paint
are exposed to it.

Points that decide the result:

- **Colour variation.** Kota varies between and within lots. Mixing slabs from
  several crates before laying avoids visible patches.
- **Movement joints.** Joints in the structure and long runs of floor need
  [movement joints](/glossary/movement-joint) carried through the bed and the
  stone, or cracks appear along them.
- **The base.** A hollow or cracked base shows through as cracked slabs. The
  level and condition of the [screed or base below](/glossary/screed) matter as
  much as the stone.

## Testing and specification

Limestone slabs and tiles are specified under **IS 1128**, which covers
dimensions, tolerances and quality of the stone. Water absorption and porosity
are tested under **IS 1124**, and resistance to wear by abrasion under
**IS 1706**. For a large or demanding floor, asking for test results on the
supplied lot, rather than general quarry literature, is reasonable, because
stone varies between blocks.

## Sealing, staining and maintenance

Kota is reasonably dense for a limestone, but it is not impervious. In pantries,
canteens and washrooms:

- **Seal it** with a penetrating sealer suited to limestone, applied after
  grinding and once the floor is dry, and renew it on the manufacturer's cycle
- **Use pH-neutral cleaners.** Acidic toilet cleaners and descalers etch the
  surface; harsh alkalis can also damage sealers
- **Wipe oil and food spills quickly**, before they soak in

Etching and wear can usually be ground out, which is Kota's real advantage over
a tile: the floor can be renewed in place.

## Kota stone compared with vitrified tile and other natural stone

Against [vitrified tile](/materials/vitrified-tile), Kota is heavier, thicker,
more absorbent and more sensitive to acids, but it can be reground when worn and
does not chip at the edges the way a tile can. Vitrified tile is more uniform,
more stain resistant, laid thinner on adhesive, and offers far more colours and
formats. Against granite and marble used as
[natural stone cladding and flooring](/materials/natural-stone-cladding), Kota is
plainer and usually cheaper; granite is harder and more acid resistant, while
marble etches even more readily. For how these finishes fit an office floor as a
whole, see [flooring systems](/services/interior-fit-out/flooring-systems).

## Common mistakes

- **Polished Kota at entrances and on ramps.** It is slippery once wet; use a
  natural or rough finish where rain or washing reaches the floor.
- **Slabs bedded on dabs of mortar.** Unsupported slabs sound hollow and crack.
- **Laying from one crate at a time.** The colour shift shows as bands across
  the floor.
- **Acidic cleaners in washrooms.** Dull etched patches appear within weeks.
- **Grinding after joinery and paint.** Slurry splashes finished work.
- **No movement joints.** Cracks follow the structural joints anyway.

## What to ask your contractor or supplier

- Which finish is proposed for each area, and why?
- What thickness and slab size, and from which lot?
- Will slabs be mixed across crates before laying?
- Is the bed full, and where are movement joints placed?
- Which sealer, when is it applied, and what cleaning regime follows?

## Standards referenced

Limestone slabs and tiles under **IS 1128**; water absorption and porosity of
natural building stones under **IS 1124**; resistance to wear by abrasion under
**IS 1706**. Finish selection for slip-prone areas, slab thickness for heavier
duties and the placing of movement joints must be confirmed by the project's
designer and engineer.
