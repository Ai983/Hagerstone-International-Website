## What the board choice turns on

Behind every laminate, lacquer or veneer in an office sits a board, and that
board decides whether the furniture is still square, flat and holding its
hinges in five years. The four common choices are **plywood, MDF, HDHMR and
particle board**. The misconception is that one of them is simply "better".
Each fails in a different way, so the right answer is usually a mix: the board
chosen for each part by what that part has to do.

Getting it wrong is expensive because furniture problems arrive after
occupation: shelves that sag under files, shutters that swell beside the pantry
sink, hinges that pull out of a door edge. Replacing joinery in a working
office means downtime and a second procurement cycle, which is often the
longest lead item; see
[office furniture procurement lead times](/blog/office-furniture-procurement-lead-times).

## The four boards compared

| Property | Plywood | MDF | HDHMR | Particle board |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Make-up | Cross-laminated veneers | Fine wood fibres and resin | Denser fibres, moisture-resistant resin | Wood chips and particles with resin |
| Indian Standard | IS 303 / IS 710 | IS 12406 | Usually sold against MDF grades or maker's data | IS 3087 |
| Screw holding (face) | Best | Moderate | Good | Lowest |
| Screw holding (edge) | Good | Weak | Moderate | Very weak |
| Moisture behaviour | Grade dependent; edges still need sealing | Raw edges swell permanently | Swells less; still not for wet areas | Swells badly at raw edges |
| Routing and paint finish | Grain and voids can show | Excellent, smooth, profiles cleanly | Good | Poor for routing; laminate only |
| Flatness for large panels | Can bow if unbalanced | Very flat | Very flat | Flat |
| Relative cost | Highest | Moderate | Moderate to high | Lowest |

**IS 12406** covers MDF for general purposes in regular, moisture-resistant
(MR) and high moisture-resistant (HMR) grades, with the grade chosen by the
humidity the board will see. **IS 3087** covers medium density particle board.
In European specifications the equivalents are **EN 622-5** for MDF and
**EN 312** for particle board, which also classes boards by load-bearing and
humid-condition use.

## Where each fits in office furniture and panelling

- **Plywood** for carcasses that carry hardware, shelves with longer spans,
  doors with heavy hinges, and desk tops. Grades are explained in
  [plywood grades for interiors](/materials/plywood-grades-for-interiors).
- **MDF** for painted or lacquered shutters, routed and grooved panels, curved
  or profiled elements, and wall panelling that must be perfectly flat. Many
  perforated and slotted
  [acoustic wall panels](/materials/acoustic-wall-panel) use an MDF base for the
  same reason.
- **HDHMR** where MDF's finish is wanted but the space is humid, such as panels
  near glazing, AC-cooled rooms with frequent shutdowns, or pantry upper
  cabinets away from direct water.
- **Particle board** for budget carcasses in dry areas, factory-laminated and
  edge-banded, where loads and hinge use are light.

Typical thicknesses differ by part and by board, because MDF and particle board
need more thickness than plywood to span the same shelf. The furniture
designer and the board manufacturer's data set the thickness for each part.

## Edge sealing and fixings

Almost every MDF and particle board failure starts at an **edge**:

- **Edge banding** on every exposed edge, applied by machine with a continuous
  glue line, not hand-glued strips with gaps
- **Sealing hidden edges** such as cut-outs for sinks, cable grommets and
  hinge pockets, which are where water enters first
- **Balanced faces**: laminate or veneer on both sides so the panel does not
  bow; see [laminate and veneer for joinery](/materials/laminate-and-veneer)
- **Fixings made for the board**: pre-drilled holes, coarse-thread or
  purpose-made screws, inserts or knock-down connectors at joints that are
  taken apart, and hinges set back from edges

## Formaldehyde and handover

MDF, HDHMR and particle board are bonded with resins that can release
formaldehyde, most when new. Release is measured by a chamber method such as
**EN 717-1** and the board sold against an emission class. Specify a low
emission class, ask for the report, and allow the furniture to air before the
office is occupied.

## Common mistakes

- **One board for everything.** Particle board shelves sag; MDF door edges
  lose their hinges.
- **MDF under the pantry sink.** The base swells within months and cannot be
  repaired.
- **Screws into raw board edges** without inserts or pre-drilling.
- **Hand-applied edge banding** with gaps that let humidity in.
- **"HDHMR" accepted on the name alone**, with no grade or swelling data.
- **Painted MDF without sealing the cut edges**, so the paint cracks along every
  profile.

## What to ask your furniture supplier

- Which board is used for each part: carcass, shelf, shutter, top?
- Which standard and grade is each board, and is there a test report?
- What is the formaldehyde emission class?
- How are edges banded and hidden edges sealed?
- Which hinges and connectors are used, and are they rated for this board?

## Standards referenced

MDF under **IS 12406** and **EN 622-5**; particle board under **IS 3087** and
**EN 312**; plywood under IS 303 and IS 710. Formaldehyde release by the chamber
method under **EN 717-1**. The board, grade and thickness for each furniture
part should be confirmed by the project's interior designer and the furniture
manufacturer's technical data.
